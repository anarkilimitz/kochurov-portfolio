PART ONE

THE LANGUAGE

PRODUCT

Backbase is a white-label digital banking platform. Banks license the front end, theme it in their own brand, and ship it to their customers as their app. 150+ banks ran on it during my time — AIB, Barclays, Citibank, Banorte, Discovery Bank, First National Bank. The product line spans retail banking, business banking, onboarding and wealth, plus the employee-facing workspaces bank staff work in behind them.

Two consequences follow from that, and everything in this case comes out of them. The bank is buying the surface, so every sales demo is a design demo. And every bank that signs is a billable implementation, because the front end has to be rebuilt in that bank's brand before it can go live. The design layer is the product.

SITUATION

Deals that should have closed were slowing down. Design quality kept surfacing in those conversations — never the headline objection, but often enough to notice. The company took it seriously enough to commission research with real users, asking directly why prospects were not buying.

So the question arrived at design as a diagnostic: what is actually wrong with what we are selling?

One complication sat underneath it. The design system itself was not trusted internally. Teams treated it as a library to pick from rather than a tool to design with. Whatever the research found, fixing it was never going to be a matter of shipping better components.

FINDINGS

1 · No flexibility to express a bank's brand. A bank licenses the front end in order to ship its own app. In practice the theming layer could not carry a brand far enough — the colour and the logo changed, the character did not. Every implementation came out recognisably the same product underneath. That breaks the promise on the exact thing the platform is sold on, and it is the hardest objection to answer in a demo, because the prospect is looking at another bank's app while you make the claim.

Observed across implementations. Not measured — the proof came later, in the fix.

2 · Fragmented styles across the product lines. Retail, business banking, onboarding and wealth had drifted into separate visual dialects. Typography did not share a system. The asset library carried several styles at once. Dark mode was bolted on wherever someone had needed it. In a demo that reads as one conclusion: there is no system behind this. A bank is buying a platform, and the promise of a platform is that whatever gets added next will fit.

Observed across the product lines. Not measured — reported back from sales.

3 · Product looked old, and that made it hard to sell. This one was tested rather than asserted. Sessions on the UserTesting platform with real users, our flows and competitor flows through an identical protocol: Cards scored 5.30 overall and 4.86 on brand alignment, Transactions 5.12 and 4.46, against Revolut at 5.60 / 5.52 on a 0–6 scale. Below the market on both core flows — and the gap on brand alignment is roughly double the gap on overall quality.

Real users, external platform, competitor-benchmarked, and re-run with the same instrument after the login MVP.

All three have one cause. Too uniform across banks, not uniform enough across our own product lines, and measurably weakest on brand — because there was no shared foundation underneath any of it.

PROBLEM

Before the Design Language initiative, our product exhibited multiple inconsistencies that hindered scalability and weakened both user and business outcomes. Key issues included:

  • Typography inconsistencies across products and flows
  • Usability problems due to legacy patterns and overloaded layouts
  • Fragmented visual asset library and mixed-quality illustrations
  • Weak dark mode that didn’t meet quality or accessibility standards
  • Overly white-label look that prevented banks from expressing their brand character
  • Overloaded screens, often broken into too many components and interactions
  • Insufficient accessibility, falling short of WCAG standards

CHALLENGES

Creating a unified Design Language for a large product ecosystem required balancing ambition with practicality. The challenge was twofold:

We had to address deep-rooted structural problems (visual fragmentation, outdated patterns, brand disconnect).

We had to implement improvements without disrupting ongoing product delivery.

We adopted an iterative, agile strategy: instead of redesigning everything at once, we focused on high-impact areas and gradually applied new principles across the product. By consolidating visual audits, usability findings, interaction gaps, and brand insights into one strategic framework, we created a strong visual and usability concept that defined the product direction and guided future decision-making.

RESEARCH

I conducted a broad competitive and market analysis focusing on leading fintech and digital banking applications. The goal was to understand how competitors approached usability, visual hierarchy, interaction patterns, and brand expression—and to identify gaps within our own product.

We evaluated Backbase’s core flows using visual scoring and benchmarked them against award-winning apps, competitors and best design solutions. The results confirmed key weaknesses in typography, spacing, visual rhythm, and layout density. These metrics provided clear targets for improvement and helped align stakeholders around the need for a unified Design Language.

INSIGHTS

Research made it clear: incremental updates wouldn’t fix systemic issues.
We responded with a comprehensive design language, which delivered:

Usability gains. A modernized Login flow, enhanced with QR-based payment initiation and streamlined entry points.

Higher visual quality. Visual scoring improved from 4.86 → 5.69 (Backbase metrics) and 5.30 → 5.87 overall, general screens core flows.

Smarter component strategy. New component and widget patterns increased flexibility and reduced redundancy across products.

Faster delivery. Client implementations accelerated by nearly 2×, thanks to more coherent components and new design strategies gave this flexibility.

Together, these changes created a more stable, scalable, and brand-expressive foundation for the product ecosystem.

RESULTS

Splash Screen
By refining the splash screen experience, we enhance both design quality and business value. Our approach ensures a seamless transition between app states, reducing unnecessary delays and improving perceived performance. The themeable splash screen allows banks to create a unique brand presence while maintaining consistency across different platforms. By optimizing animations and transitions, we strike a balance between branding impact and speed, preventing user frustration. These improvements lead to higher user retention, increased engagement, and a more polished product experience — ultimately driving customer satisfaction and strengthening the bank’s competitive positioning.

Impact:
Higher clarity, reduced friction, improved usability metrics, and alignment with modern authentication expectations (e.g., QR-based payment initiation, biometrics).

Login screen We streamlined the login experience by removing unnecessary elements, ensuring a clear and focused process. The design now highlights two primary entry points, making access more intuitive. Additionally, we introduced supportive features, such as direct contact with the bank and branch locator options, providing users with greater flexibility and control over their banking experience.

Based on insight from the scoring framework, we identified problematic screens (e.g., login, authentication flows) and reworked them using the new principles. This led to stronger hierarchy, improved usability, and a more expressive, brand-aligned visual system.

Final summary and next steps
The design language established a scalable foundation for Backbase products — addressing inconsistencies, improving usability, strengthening brand expression, and reducing implementation complexity.
